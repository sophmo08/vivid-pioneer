abstract: 'Occupational gender segregation remains a defining feature of labor markets worldwide, and efforts to recruit individuals into gender-atypical occupations often fail because newcomers drop out shortly after entry. Understanding what drives this early attrition requires examining the day-to-day experiences of newcomers whose gender is underrepresented in their occupation, especially during the critical first weeks of organizational socialization. Integrating role congruity theory with a dynamic work-events perspective, we propose that gender-atypical newcomers experience an intensifying trajectory of perceived negative work events over time, and that this intensifying trajectory undermines their task mastery and heightens their turnover intention. Results from a longitudinal field study applying a repeated-measures design to 673 newcomers across 88 occupations over 12 weeks support these predictions. Newcomers working in gender-atypical occupations experienced a significant increase in negative work events over time, with this effect being significantly stronger for men in female-dominated occupations than for women in male-dominated occupations. Furthermore, steeper negative work event trajectories were associated with lower task mastery and higher turnover intentions. These findings advance dissimilarity research by uncovering the dynamic mechanism through which gender dissimilarity translates into adverse adjustment outcomes and contribute to the anchoring events literature by demonstrating the disproportionate and lasting influence of early negative experiences at the very outset of a professional career.'
